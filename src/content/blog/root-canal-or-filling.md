It's one of the most common questions patients arrive with: *"Is this just a filling, or am I in root canal territory?"* The answer comes down to one thing — how deep the problem has gone.

## The one-line answer

A **filling** repairs damage to the outer layers of the tooth (enamel and dentine). A **root canal** is needed when decay or injury has reached the **pulp** — the living nerve and blood supply inside the tooth — and it has become inflamed or infected.

## Signs that point to a filling

- Sensitivity to sweets or cold that **goes away within seconds** of removing the trigger
- A visible cavity or food catching in a tooth, without much pain
- Mild, occasional discomfort when chewing

At this stage the nerve is still healthy. Clean out the decay, place a filling, done — usually in one short visit.

## Signs that point to a root canal

- Pain that **lingers for minutes** after hot or cold, or arrives on its own
- Throbbing pain that wakes you at night
- Pain on biting or pressing the tooth
- A tooth that has darkened compared to its neighbours
- Swelling or a pimple-like bump on the gum near the tooth

These suggest the pulp is inflamed or already infected. A filling placed over an infected nerve doesn't fix anything — the infection continues underneath, which is why proper diagnosis matters more than optimism.

## The honest grey zone

Deep decay that sits *close* to the nerve is a judgement call. Sometimes a deep filling with a protective liner works and the nerve settles; sometimes the nerve was already too far gone and a root canal follows weeks later. A good dentist will tell you when you're in this zone rather than promising certainty.

Diagnosis takes minutes: we look at the tooth, test how it responds, and take an X-ray that shows how close the decay is to the nerve and whether there's infection at the root tip.
