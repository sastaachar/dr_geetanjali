That sharp jolt when cold water hits a tooth is one of the most common complaints we hear. Most of the time it is ordinary sensitivity and it is fixable. Sometimes it is the early warning of something that needs treating. The difference is easy to tell once you know what to listen for.

## What sensitivity actually is

Under your enamel is a layer called dentine, and dentine is not solid. It is threaded with microscopic tubes that lead to the nerve. While enamel and gum cover it, nothing gets through. When dentine is exposed, cold, heat and sweetness travel down those tubes and the nerve reports it as a jolt.

So sensitivity is not a disease in itself. It is a sign that dentine has been uncovered somewhere.

## The usual reasons dentine gets exposed

**Brushing too hard, with the wrong brush.** This is the most common cause by a distance, and the most misunderstood. Scrubbing sideways with a hard brush wears a notch into the tooth right at the gumline and pushes the gum back at the same time. Plaque comes off with a soft brush and gentle circles. It never needed force.

**Receding gums.** The root surface has no enamel on it at all, so any root that becomes exposed is sensitive by default. Gum disease, hard brushing and simple ageing all cause recession.

**Grinding and clenching.** Often done in your sleep, this wears through enamel over the years and flexes the tooth at the neck. If you wake with a tight jaw or your partner mentions the noise, this is likely part of it.

**Acid.** Citrus, fizzy drinks, vinegar-heavy food and frequent sipping through the day dissolve enamel. Acid reflux does the same thing from the inside, and often hits the back teeth.

**Recent dental work or whitening.** Sensitivity after a filling, a cleaning or a whitening session is normal and settles within days or a couple of weeks. We covered this in [whether whitening damages teeth](/blog/does-teeth-whitening-damage-teeth/).

## The signs that it is not just sensitivity

This is the part worth remembering. Ordinary sensitivity is **sharp, immediate and brief**. The pain arrives with the cold and it leaves within a second or two of the cold going away.

Get the tooth looked at if instead:

- The pain **lingers** for thirty seconds or more after the cold is gone
- It starts **on its own**, with nothing touching it
- It wakes you at night, or is worse when you lie down
- **Heat** sets it off, and cold water actually relieves it
- It is one specific tooth rather than a general area
- The tooth hurts when you **bite** on it

Lingering pain, spontaneous pain and pain that heat triggers all suggest the nerve inside the tooth is inflamed or dying rather than merely exposed. That is a cavity reaching the nerve, a crack, or a tooth that needs a root canal, and none of those improve on their own. ## What actually helps ordinary sensitivity

**Use a desensitising toothpaste properly.** Most people use it wrong. Do not rinse it away. Brush with it, spit, and leave the residue on the teeth. Better still, rub a smear onto the sensitive spot with a fingertip at bedtime and leave it overnight. Give it two to four weeks before judging whether it works.

**Change how you brush, not how hard.** Soft bristles, small circles, no scrubbing. If you press hard enough to splay the bristles, you are pressing far too hard.

**Wait thirty minutes after anything acidic** before brushing. Enamel is temporarily softened right after acid, and brushing immediately scrubs it away.

**Deal with the grinding.** A night guard protects the teeth from a habit you cannot consciously stop.

## When to come in

If sensitivity has lasted more than a few weeks despite a desensitising toothpaste, or if any of the warning signs above apply, book an examination. The cause is usually visible: a worn notch, an area of recession, a cracked filling or a cavity. Treatment depends entirely on which one it is, and guessing is what turns a small fix into a root canal.
