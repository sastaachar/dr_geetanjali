Short answer: **professionally supervised whitening does not damage enamel.** Decades of studies on carbamide and hydrogen peroxide whitening show no meaningful harm to enamel structure when used correctly. But "used correctly" is carrying weight in that sentence — so let's separate the safe from the risky.

## What whitening actually does

Whitening gels release oxygen molecules that travel into the enamel and break up the stain compounds trapped there. It's a chemical process, not an abrasive one — nothing is scraped or etched away. This is why properly done whitening can lighten teeth several shades without changing the tooth surface.

## The side effect that is real: temporary sensitivity

Around half of whitening patients feel some sensitivity during or shortly after treatment — a zing to cold that fades within a day or two. It happens because the pores of the enamel are temporarily more open. It's uncomfortable, not harmful, and manageable: we adjust gel strength and timing, and a desensitising toothpaste before and after treatment makes a real difference.

## Where whitening *can* go wrong

**Overuse of home kits.** The horror stories almost always involve unsupervised, repeated use of strong gels bought online — daily use for weeks, gums burning, teeth translucent at the edges. More is not whiter; past a point, you're just irritating the nerve and the gums.

**Whitening over cavities or leaking fillings.** Gel entering a cavity hurts badly and inflames the nerve. This is the strongest argument for a check-up before whitening — one look and an X-ray rules it out.

**Lemon, charcoal and other DIY "whitening".** Acidic and abrasive home remedies genuinely do erode enamel — permanently. Everything whitening gel is accused of, lemon juice actually does.

**Expecting whitening to work on crowns and fillings.** It only works on natural teeth. Existing crowns keep their colour, which is why we whiten first and match new restorations after.

## One more thing: cleaning often gets you halfway

A lot of "yellowing" is surface stain from tea, coffee and tobacco sitting on tartar. [A professional cleaning and polish](/treatments/teeth-cleaning/) removes that layer — some patients look at the mirror afterwards and decide they don't need whitening at all.
