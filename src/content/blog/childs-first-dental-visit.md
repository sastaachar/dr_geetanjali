Most children in India arrive at a dental clinic for the first time because something hurts. That is late, and it sets up a lifelong association between dentists and pain. A first visit that happens before there is a problem is shorter, cheaper and far less frightening.

## When to come

The guidance is **the first birthday, or within six months of the first tooth appearing**, whichever comes first.

That sounds absurdly early to most parents, and it is fair to ask what there is to examine in a mouth with two teeth. The answer is that the first visit is mostly not about the teeth. It is about spotting early decay risk, checking how the jaws and bite are developing, sorting out feeding and brushing habits, and, most valuably, letting the child meet a dentist in a visit where nothing is done to them.

A child whose first four visits were painless does not develop dental fear. That is the entire strategy.

## "They are only milk teeth, they will fall out anyway"

This is the most common reason treatment gets postponed, and it is wrong in four specific ways.

**They are not all temporary in the way you think.** The back milk molars stay until roughly age eleven or twelve. A cavity at age five in that tooth has six or seven years to cause trouble.

**Decay in a milk tooth can damage the permanent tooth forming underneath it.** An abscess on a milk molar sits directly above the developing premolar and can leave it discoloured or malformed.

**Milk teeth hold the space.** If one is lost too early, the neighbouring teeth drift into the gap and the permanent tooth arrives to find nowhere to go. A significant amount of childhood orthodontic crowding traces back to a milk molar extracted years earlier without a space maintainer.

**Toothache affects a child's daily life.** Children with untreated decay eat less, sleep worse and miss school. They also frequently do not report the pain in a way adults recognise, and simply chew on one side instead.

## What actually happens at a first visit

Very little, deliberately. A typical first appointment is fifteen to twenty minutes:

- The child sits in the chair, often on a parent's lap for the youngest ones, and gets a ride up and down
- We count the teeth and look for early white-spot lesions, which are decay in its reversible stage
- We check the bite, and habits like thumb sucking or mouth breathing
- We show you the brushing technique on the child's own teeth, which is more useful than any explanation
- We talk about diet, particularly the frequency of sugar rather than the quantity
- Where useful, a fluoride application, which takes a minute and does not require drilling

No injections, no drilling, no restraint. If the child is not comfortable, we stop and try again another day. Nothing about a first visit is worth winning an argument over.

## How to prepare your child, and what not to say

This matters more than anything the clinic does.

**Avoid the word "pain", including in a denial.** "It will not hurt" plants the idea that hurting was on the table. So does "be brave" and "you will not need an injection". Children hear the noun, not the negation.

**Do not use the dentist as a threat.** "Eat your sweets and the dentist will pull your teeth out" is a common line and it costs us the child's trust before they arrive.

**Do not transfer your own fear.** If you dread dental visits, send the parent who does not, and keep your commentary in the waiting room neutral.

**Do describe it plainly and positively:** the dentist is going to count your teeth and check they are strong. Frame it as something interesting rather than something to endure.

**Book a morning appointment** for small children. A tired child at 7 pm is a different child.

## After the first visit

Every six months for a check-up, unless we suggest otherwise. Brush twice a day with a smear of fluoride toothpaste, and keep brushing for them until they are about seven, since younger children genuinely lack the hand control to do it properly no matter how willing they are.
